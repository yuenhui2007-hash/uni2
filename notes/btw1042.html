BTW1042 – Business Law

Deep concepts: Malaysian legal system, contract, negligence, agency, structures

1. Malaysian Legal System – Structure and Sources

Malaysia has a dual legal system influenced by English common law and local legislation, with Islamic law applying in personal matters for Muslims. The Federal Constitution is the supreme law; any law inconsistent with it is void to the extent of the inconsistency.

Sources of law:

  • Written law – Constitution, Acts of Parliament, state enactments, subsidiary legislation
  • Unwritten law – English common law and rules of equity (where applicable and suitable to local circumstances), judicial precedent, customs

Courts apply the doctrine of binding precedent (stare decisis): lower courts follow decisions of higher courts on similar points of law. Hierarchy (simplified): Federal Court → Court of Appeal → High Court → Subordinate Courts (Sessions, Magistrates).

2. Contract Law – Formation in Depth

A contract is an agreement enforceable by law. Under the Contracts Act 1950 (and common-law principles), the main building blocks are:

Offer and Acceptance

Offer – A definite proposal made with the intention that it shall become binding as soon as it is accepted. It must be communicated to the offeree.
Invitation to treat – An indication of willingness to negotiate (e.g. goods on display, advertisements, catalogues, auctioneer calling for bids). It is not an offer; the customer’s response is usually the offer.

Acceptance must be absolute and unqualified (mirror-image rule). A purported acceptance that adds new terms is a counter-offer, which rejects the original offer. Acceptance must be communicated (silence is generally not acceptance), subject to limited exceptions (e.g. postal rule in appropriate cases).

Consideration, Intention, Capacity, Certainty

  • Consideration – Something of value in the eyes of the law given in exchange. It must be sufficient but need not be adequate. Past consideration is generally not good consideration.
  • Intention to create legal relations – Commercial agreements are presumed to have it; social/domestic agreements are presumed not to, unless rebutted.
  • Capacity – Minors, persons of unsound mind and some others have restricted capacity; contracts may be voidable or void.
  • Certainty – Terms must be sufficiently clear to be enforced.

Vitiating Factors

Even if formation elements appear present, consent may be flawed: misrepresentation (innocent, negligent, fraudulent), mistake, coercion, undue influence, or illegality. Consequences range from rescission to damages or the contract being void/voidable depending on the factor and statute.

3. Tort of Negligence – Elements in Depth

Negligence protects against careless infliction of harm. The claimant must establish:

  1. Duty of care – A legal duty owed to the claimant. In novel situations, courts consider foreseeability, proximity and whether it is fair, just and reasonable to impose a duty. Professionals (e.g. accountants) owe a duty to clients and, in limited cases, to third parties who rely on their work.
  2. Breach – Failure to meet the standard of the reasonable person (or reasonably competent professional in that field).
  3. Causation – Factual causation (“but for” the breach, the loss would not have occurred) and legal causation (no intervening act that breaks the chain).
  4. Damage not too remote – The type of loss must be reasonably foreseeable.

Defences may include contributory negligence and voluntary assumption of risk. For business students, the practical message is: take reasonable care in advice and operations; document processes; manage reliance by third parties.

4. Agency

Agency allows one person (agent) to affect the legal position of another (principal) in relation to third parties. Authority may be actual (express or implied) or apparent (estoppel based on the principal’s representation). The principal is bound when the agent acts within authority. Agent duties include following instructions, care and skill, avoiding conflict of interest, and accounting. Principal duties include remuneration and indemnity as agreed.
