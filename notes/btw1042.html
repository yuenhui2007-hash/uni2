BTW1042 – Business Law

Malaysian legal system and core business law principles (contract, tort, agency, business structures).

1. Unit Overview

Designed for non-lawyers. Focuses on practical legal principles that affect business decisions in Malaysia, with comparative references to other common-law systems. You learn how to form valid contracts, manage legal risk, avoid negligence liability, and choose appropriate business entities.

2. Key Definitions

Contract
An agreement between two or more parties that is enforceable by law. In Malaysia governed primarily by the Contracts Act 1950.
Offer
A definite proposal made by one party (offeror) to another (offeree) with the intention that it shall become binding as soon as it is accepted.
Acceptance
An unqualified expression of assent to the terms of an offer. Must be communicated (exceptions exist for instantaneous communications and postal rule).
Consideration
Something of value given by each party (a promise, act or forbearance). Must be sufficient but need not be adequate. Past consideration is generally not valid.
Tort of Negligence
Failure to take reasonable care which results in damage to another person. Elements: duty of care, breach of duty, causation, and damage that is not too remote.
Agency
A relationship where one person (agent) is authorised to act on behalf of another (principal) and can create legal relations with third parties.

3. Core Concepts

A. Malaysian Legal System

  • Federal Constitution is the supreme law.
  • Sources of law: written law (Constitution, statutes, subsidiary legislation), unwritten law (common law, equity, judicial precedent, customs).
  • Court hierarchy: Federal Court → Court of Appeal → High Court → Sessions Court → Magistrates’ Court.
  • Doctrine of binding precedent (stare decisis).

B. Essential Elements of a Valid Contract

  1. Offer
  2. Acceptance
  3. Consideration
  4. Intention to create legal relations
  5. Capacity (age, sound mind)
  6. Free consent (no coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation, mistake)
  7. Lawful object and consideration
  8. Certainty of terms

C. Vitiating Factors

  • Misrepresentation (innocent, negligent, fraudulent)
  • Mistake
  • Duress / Coercion
  • Undue influence
  • Illegality

D. Business Structures

  • Sole proprietorship – simplest, unlimited liability.
  • Partnership – two or more persons, unlimited liability (unless limited partnership), governed by Partnership Act 1961.
  • Company – separate legal entity, limited liability, governed by Companies Act 2016.

E. Agency

  • Creation: express, implied, ratification, necessity, estoppel.
  • Agent’s duties: follow instructions, care and skill, no conflict of interest, account for money.
  • Principal’s duties: remuneration, indemnity.
  • Types of authority: actual (express/implied), apparent (ostensible).

4. Worked Examples / Application

Scenario – Is there a contract?
A shop displays a laptop priced at RM2,500. Customer takes it to the counter. Is the price tag an offer?
Answer: Generally no – it is an invitation to treat. The customer makes the offer by presenting the item; the shop accepts by taking payment.
Scenario – Negligence
An accountant gives careless tax advice to a client who relies on it and suffers loss. Can the client sue?
Answer: Yes, if duty of care is established (professional relationship), the advice fell below the standard of a reasonably competent accountant, and the loss was caused by the advice and not too remote.

5. Exam Tips

  • Use the IRAC method: Issue → Rule → Application → Conclusion.
  • Always state the relevant section of the Contracts Act 1950 or Companies Act 2016 when possible.
  • Distinguish clearly between invitation to treat and offer.
  • For negligence, go through all four elements systematically.
  • Compare sole prop / partnership / company on liability, continuity and regulation.
