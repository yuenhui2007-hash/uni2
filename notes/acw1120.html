1. Why financial accounting exists

Outside investors and lenders cannot observe the firm’s books daily. Financial accounting measures economic events, classifies them into elements, and communicates them in general-purpose financial statements. Those statements support decisions to buy, sell or hold equity and debt, and to extend or settle credit. They also support assessment of management’s stewardship of resources.

Agency conflict: managers (agents) may pursue goals that diverge from owners/creditors (principals). Accounting numbers enter debt covenants, bonus contracts and valuation models — so recognition and measurement choices have real economic consequences, not just “presentation”.

Accrual vs cash: Cash accounting records only cash movements and can misstate performance (e.g. large advance receipts look like profit). Accrual accounting recognises effects when they occur (revenue when earned; expenses when incurred). The Conceptual Framework bases GPFS on accruals.

2. Conceptual Framework (exam core)

High exam weightQuote definitions accurately. Markers award marks for Framework wording, not paraphrases that change meaning.
Objective: Provide financial information about the reporting entity that is useful to existing and potential investors, lenders and other creditors in making decisions about providing resources to the entity (including assessing stewardship).

2.1 Qualitative characteristics

For information to be useful it must meet the fundamental characteristics. Enhancing characteristics improve usefulness only if the fundamentals are already present.

Fundamental

Relevance

  • Can change decisions of primary users
  • Predictive and/or confirmatory value
  • Materiality = entity-specific relevance
  • Omit / misstate / obscure → could reasonably influence users (by size or nature)
Fundamental

Faithful representation

  • Shows the substance of economic phenomena
  • As complete as practicable
  • Neutral (no bias)
  • Free from error as far as practicable — not “perfectly accurate”
Enhancing

Four enhancing characteristics

Comparability
Including consistency over time and across entities
Verifiability
Different knowledgeable observers could reach similar consensus
Timeliness
Information available in time to influence decisions
Understandability
Clear classification and presentation for users with reasonable knowledge
Exam trade-off: a quick estimate available tomorrow can be more useful than a precise figure available next month — relevance vs perfect precision.
Self-check — close your notes
  1. Name the two fundamental qualitative characteristics.
  2. What is materiality in one sentence?
  3. List the four enhancing characteristics.
  4. True/False: enhancing characteristics can replace missing relevance.

2.2 Elements — learn exact wording

Asset

A present economic resource controlled by the entity as a result of past events.

Economic resource = a right that has the potential to produce economic benefits.

Liability

A present obligation of the entity to transfer an economic resource as a result of past events.

The entity has no practical ability to avoid the obligation.

Equity

The residual interest in the assets of the entity after deducting all its liabilities.

Equity = Assets − Liabilities

Income

Increases in assets, or decreases in liabilities, that result in increases in equity — other than contributions from owners.

Expenses

Decreases in assets, or increases in liabilities, that result in decreases in equity — other than distributions to owners.

Recognition decision: the item meets the definition of an element and recognising it provides useful information (relevant + faithfully representable). Extreme measurement uncertainty or a very low probability of inflow/outflow may mean disclosure only, not recognition.

2.3 Measurement bases (awareness)

Historical cost, current value (e.g. fair value, value in use), amortised cost. Cost is verifiable but may be less relevant when prices move; fair value may be relevant but Level-3 inputs are less verifiable.

3. Accounting equation and double-entry

Assets = Liabilities + Equity
Equity = Capital + Income − Expenses − Drawings

Every journal entry keeps the equation in balance: total debits = total credits.

4. Accounting cycle

  1. Identify transactions from source documents
  2. Analyse effect on equation → choose accounts
  3. Journalise (chronological; include narration)
  4. Post to ledger (T-accounts)
  5. Unadjusted trial balance (Dr totals = Cr totals only)
  6. Adjusting entries
  7. Adjusted trial balance
  8. Financial statements
  9. Closing entries (temporary → equity)
  10. Post-closing trial balance (permanent accounts only)

Critical: A balanced trial balance does not prove the books are correct. It misses: omitted transactions, equal wrong amounts, errors of principle (e.g. expense capitalised), compensating errors.

5. Adjusting entries (highest exam weight)

Highest practical weightAlmost every exam has adjusting entries. Never use Cash in a pure adjusting entry.

Purpose: allocate income/expenses to the correct period under accruals; update assets and liabilities.

Do notPut Cash in a pure adjusting entry. Adjustments allocate amounts already recorded (or not yet recorded) — they are not cash movements.
  1. Identify what has been used up, earned, incurred, or not yet billed at year-end.
  2. Decide which asset/liability and which expense/income accounts move.
  3. Journalise with narration and amounts from clear workings (fractions of months).
  4. Post and carry into the adjusted trial balance → financial statements.

6. Depreciation in depth

Depreciation is allocation of cost, not valuation to market. Depreciable amount = Cost − Residual value. Accumulated depreciation is a contra-asset. Carrying amount = Cost − Accum. dep.

Straight-line annual = (Cost − Residual) / Useful life in years
For part-year: annual × months in use / 12 (if policy is monthly)
Cost 120,000; residual 12,000; life 9 years; bought 1 April; year-end 31 Dec.
Annual = 108,000/9 = 12,000; monthly = 1,000
Y1 (9 months): expense 9,000; carrying amount = 120,000 − 9,000 = 111,000
Y2: expense 12,000; carrying amount = 99,000

Changes in residual value or useful life = change in estimate → apply prospectively (do not rewrite past depreciation), unless your unit guide states otherwise.

Do notForget residual value in the depreciable amount, or credit the Equipment account for routine depreciation (credit Accumulated depreciation).
Self-check — Depreciation
  1. Cost 80,000; residual 8,000; life 8 years. Annual SL expense?
  2. What is carrying amount after 3 full years?

7. Financial statements

  • Statement of profit or loss: Income − Expenses = Profit/(Loss)
  • Statement of changes in equity: Opening equity + Profit − Drawings ± Capital = Closing equity
  • Statement of financial position: Assets = Liabilities + Equity (current / non-current split as taught)

Closing: Dr each income account / Cr Income summary (or RE); Dr Income summary / Cr each expense; transfer net profit to capital/RE; close drawings to capital/RE.

  1. Prepare adjustments and adjusted figures.
  2. Income statement: income − expenses = profit.
  3. SCE: opening equity + profit − drawings ± capital.
  4. SFP: assets = liabilities + equity (balances).
  5. Close temporary accounts to equity / income summary as taught.
Self-check — Statements
  1. Which accounts are temporary?
  2. Does a balanced trial balance prove the books are correct? Why/why not?

11. Retail inventory (service vs trading — high exam value)

Service entities have no inventory of goods for sale. Retail/trading entities buy and sell merchandise. Two recording systems:

  • Perpetual: continuous update of Inventory and COGS at each purchase/sale. Requires inventory subsidiary records.
  • Periodic: purchases recorded in Purchases account; COGS computed at period-end: Opening inventory + Net purchases − Closing inventory.
COGS (periodic) = Opening inventory + Purchases − Purchase returns − Purchase discounts + Freight-in − Closing inventory
Gross profit = Net sales − COGS
Net sales = Sales − Sales returns − Sales discounts
Periodic COGS worked example
Opening inventory 20,000; Purchases 80,000; Purchase returns 3,000; Freight-in 2,000; Closing inventory 18,000.
Net purchases = 80,000 − 3,000 + 2,000 = 79,000
COGS = 20,000 + 79,000 − 18,000 = 81,000
Perpetual sale entry (cost 600, selling price 1,000 on credit)
Dr Accounts receivable 1,000 / Cr Sales 1,000
Dr COGS 600 / Cr Inventory 600

Inventory cost flow (if taught): specific identification, FIFO, weighted average. Choice affects COGS and ending inventory when unit costs change — and therefore profit. In rising prices, FIFO → lower COGS, higher profit vs weighted average (direction depends on method set).

12. Special journals and control accounts (if in your week plan)

High-volume entities use special journals (sales, purchases, cash receipts, cash payments) plus general journal for adjustments/non-routine entries. Control accounts (AR, AP, Inventory) in the general ledger are supported by subsidiary ledgers. Trial balance uses control account balances; subsidiary schedules must reconcile to controls.

13. Bad debts / allowance method (common standard topic)

Receivables should be reported at the amount expected to be collected. Under the allowance method:

  • Estimate uncollectible amounts → Dr Bad debt expense / Cr Allowance for doubtful debts (contra-asset)
  • Write-off specific account → Dr Allowance / Cr AR (no expense at write-off if previously allowed)
AR 50,000; existing allowance credit balance 800; estimate 4% of AR uncollectible.
Required allowance = 2,000; adjustment = 2,000 − 800 = 1,200
Dr Bad debt expense 1,200 / Cr Allowance 1,200
Net receivables = 50,000 − 2,000 = 48,000

14. Bank reconciliation (practical exam staple)

Reconcile cash per books to cash per bank. Typical reconciling items:

  • Deposits in transit (+ bank side)
  • Outstanding cheques (− bank side)
  • Bank charges, NSF cheques (− book side → journalise)
  • Interest earned, note collections (+ book side → journalise)
  • Errors (side depends on who erred)

Only book-side items require journal entries. After entries, adjusted cash book = adjusted bank balance.

Bank statement 15,200; deposits in transit 1,800; outstanding cheques 2,100; bank fees 50 not recorded; interest 30 not recorded.
Adjusted bank = 15,200 + 1,800 − 2,100 = 14,900
Books before adj. must be brought to 14,900: Dr Bank fees exp 50 / Cr Cash; Dr Cash 30 / Cr Interest revenue.

15. Measurement systems vs Framework

Historical cost prioritises verifiability; current value prioritises relevance when prices change. The Framework does not prescribe a single measurement basis for all assets/liabilities. Exam questions may ask you to evaluate whether historical cost faithfully represents a particular asset after a large market move — answer using relevance vs faithful representation trade-off language.

Retail vs serviceMonash LO requires financial statements for service and retail entities. Know both perpetual and periodic COGS.
Perpetual
  • Inventory updated at every purchase and sale
  • Two entries on sale: revenue and COGS
  • Continuous Inventory balance
Periodic

18. Allowance for doubtful debts (full method)

  1. Estimate uncollectible amount (e.g. % of AR or ageing).
  2. Compare to existing allowance balance.
  3. Adjust: Dr Bad debt expense / Cr Allowance.
  4. Write-off specific account: Dr Allowance / Cr AR (no expense if already allowed).
  5. Report AR net of allowance on SFP.
AR 80,000; allowance credit balance already 1,200; estimate 5% of AR uncollectible.
Required allowance = 4,000
Adjustment = 4,000 − 1,200 = 2,800
Dr Bad debt expense 2,800 / Cr Allowance 2,800
Net receivables = 80,000 − 4,000 = 76,000
Do notExpense again at write-off under the allowance method if the amount was already provided.

Bank statement side

Books side
  • Balance per cash book

20. Error types and correction logic

Omission
Transaction never recorded — trial balance may still balance
Commission
Right type of account, wrong personal account
Principle
Wrong class (e.g. expense capitalised as asset)
Compensating
Two errors cancel — TB still balances
Original entry
Wrong amount entered on both sides equally
Exam point: A balanced trial balance does not prove correctness. List what it cannot detect.

21. Closing entries — sequence

  1. Close each income account → Income summary (or RE): Dr Income / Cr Income summary.
  2. Close each expense → Income summary: Dr Income summary / Cr Expense.
  3. Close Income summary (profit) → Capital/RE: Dr Income summary / Cr Capital.
  4. Close drawings → Capital/RE: Dr Capital / Cr Drawings.
  5. Post-closing TB contains permanent accounts only.
