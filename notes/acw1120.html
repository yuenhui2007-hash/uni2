ACW1120 – Financial Accounting 1

Degree-level notes: Conceptual Framework application, recognition/measurement judgement, full-cycle mechanics, exam-style analysis

1. Role of Financial Accounting in Capital Markets

Financial accounting is an information system that measures, processes and communicates economic information about an entity to external users who lack direct access to internal records. In a degree context you must go beyond “recording transactions”: examiners test whether you understand why particular recognition and measurement choices affect contracting, stewardship and resource-allocation decisions.

Investors and creditors use financial statements to assess amounts, timing and uncertainty of future net cash inflows. Managers are evaluated on reported performance (stewardship). Standards and the Conceptual Framework constrain opportunistic reporting while still requiring professional judgement where criteria are principles-based.

2. Conceptual Framework — Exam-Ready Depth

Objective (IASB Conceptual Framework)
Provide financial information about the reporting entity that is useful to existing and potential investors, lenders and other creditors in making decisions relating to providing resources to the entity.

Those decisions involve buying, selling or holding equity and debt instruments, and providing or settling loans and other forms of credit. Users also need information to assess management’s stewardship of the entity’s economic resources.

2.1 Qualitative characteristics — how markers expect application

Relevance requires predictive and/or confirmatory value. Materiality is an entity-specific aspect of relevance: information is material if omitting, misstating or obscuring it could reasonably be expected to influence decisions of primary users. In exam answers, do not treat materiality as a separate qualitative characteristic.

Faithful representation requires depiction of the substance of economic phenomena — as complete, neutral and free from error as possible. Neutrality means without bias in selection or presentation; it does not mean without prudence in measurement uncertainty, but prudence must not be used to deliberately understate assets/income or overstate liabilities/expenses in a way that biases the statements.

Enhancing characteristics (comparability, verifiability, timeliness, understandability) improve usefulness only when fundamental characteristics are present. Exam questions often require you to rank or trade off characteristics (e.g. timely estimate vs delayed precision).

2.2 Elements — definitional precision

Asset: A present economic resource controlled by the entity as a result of past events. An economic resource is a right that has the potential to produce economic benefits.

Liability: A present obligation of the entity to transfer an economic resource as a result of past events. An obligation is a duty or responsibility that the entity has no practical ability to avoid.

Equity: The residual interest in the assets of the entity after deducting all its liabilities.

Income / Expenses: Increases / decreases in assets or decreases / increases in liabilities that result in increases / decreases in equity, other than contributions from / distributions to equity holders.

Recognition is not automatic when a definition is met. Recognition is appropriate when it provides useful information — i.e. relevant and a faithful representation. Probability of inflow/outflow and measurement uncertainty affect whether recognition (and at what amount) is useful; extreme measurement uncertainty may support non-recognition or disclosure only.

3. Accrual Accounting and the Matching Logic

Accrual accounting recognises the effects of transactions and other events when they occur, not merely when cash is received or paid. Revenue is recognised when performance obligations are satisfied (earned); expenses when consumption of economic benefits occurs or obligations arise. This produces a profit figure that attempts to measure performance for the period, not cash movement.

Cash-basis reporting can misstate performance (e.g. cash received for future services appears as “profit”). The Framework therefore bases general-purpose financial statements on accruals. In exams, always justify adjusting entries by reference to period allocation under accrual accounting — not by “because we always adjust”.

4. Double-Entry System as an Algebraic Constraint

Assets = Liabilities + Equity

Expanded: Assets = Liabilities + Capital + Income − Expenses − Drawings/Dividends. Every recognised event preserves equality through equal debits and credits. Analysing a transaction means identifying which elements change and in which direction before writing the journal entry.

5. Accounting Cycle — Control Perspective

  1. Source documents → evidence and audit trail
  2. Journal (chronological record; compound entries allowed)
  3. Ledger posting (classification by account)
  4. Unadjusted trial balance (arithmetic check only — does not prove correctness of analysis)
  5. Adjusting entries (period-end accrual/deferral/estimates)
  6. Adjusted trial balance → financial statements
  7. Closing entries (temporary accounts → retained earnings/capital)
  8. Post-closing trial balance (permanent accounts only)

A balanced trial balance does not detect errors of principle, complete omission of a transaction, or equal wrong debits and credits.

6. Adjusting Entries — Classification and Exam Technique

Adjustments allocate income and expenses to the correct period and update assets/liabilities. Cash is never debited or credited in a pure adjusting entry.

Depreciation — conceptual points examiners probe

Depreciation is an allocation of cost, not an attempt to measure current market value. Depreciable amount = cost − residual value. Useful life and residual value are estimates; changes in estimate are applied prospectively (not retrospective restatement of prior depreciation under the usual estimate-change approach taught at this level).

Straight-line annual charge = (Cost − Residual value) / Useful life
Multi-year carrying amount: Cost 90,000; residual 10,000; life 8 years.
Annual dep. = 10,000. After 3 years: Accum. dep. = 30,000; carrying amount = 60,000.
Statement of financial position presents cost and accumulated depreciation (or carrying amount with disclosure), not “market value”.

7. Financial Statements — Articulation

  • Statement of profit or loss: performance for the period (income − expenses)
  • Statement of changes in equity: reconciles opening to closing equity (profit, drawings/dividends, capital injections, other equity movements)
  • Statement of financial position: resources and claims at a point in time; current/non-current classification where applicable

Profit closes to equity; equity appears on the SFP — the statements articulate. Exam questions may require preparation of all three from an adjusted trial balance plus additional information (e.g. drawings, inventory, prepayments).

8. Common Degree-Level Errors

  • Confusing enhancing characteristics with fundamental ones
  • Defining asset/liability in outdated “probable future benefit/outflow” only language without the current Framework’s resource/obligation wording where required
  • Putting Cash in adjusting entries
  • Crediting “Depreciation” instead of Accumulated Depreciation
  • Treating a balanced trial balance as proof that the books are correct
  • Ignoring residual value when computing straight-line depreciation
Exam technique: For Framework questions, state the precise definition, then apply it to the fact pattern (does the item meet the definition? does recognition provide useful information?). For practical questions, show workings, narrate journal entries, and link adjustments to accrual period allocation.
