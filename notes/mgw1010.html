MGW1010 – Introduction to Management

Deep concepts: functions, theories, leadership, ethics, contingency

1. Management Defined

Management is the process of planning, organising, leading and controlling the use of resources to achieve organisational goals effectively and efficiently.
  • Effectiveness – Doing the right things (goal attainment)
  • Efficiency – Doing things right (minimising resource waste)

Managers operate at different levels (top, middle, first-line) and in different functional areas, but the four functions remain a useful organising framework.

2. The Four Functions – Conceptual Content

  1. Planning – Defining goals, establishing strategy, developing plans to coordinate activities. Includes strategic, tactical and operational planning; deals with uncertainty through forecasting and scenarios.
  2. Organising – Arranging and structuring work to accomplish goals. Includes division of labour, departmentalisation, chain of command, span of control, centralisation vs decentralisation, formalisation.
  3. Leading – Working with and through people to achieve goals: motivation, communication, leadership style, team dynamics, conflict management.
  4. Controlling – Monitoring performance, comparing with goals, taking corrective action. Control systems can be feedforward, concurrent or feedback.

3. Classical Approaches – Ideas and Critique

Scientific Management (Taylor)

Focus on efficiency through scientific study of tasks, time-and-motion study, standardisation, selection and training, and wage incentives. Strength: systematic improvement of work methods. Limitation: mechanistic view of workers, neglect of social and psychological needs, assumption of one best way — problematic for knowledge work and creative tasks.

Administrative Theory (Fayol)

Identified management functions and 14 principles (e.g. unity of command, scalar chain, esprit de corps). Still shapes how we describe management; principles are guidelines, not rigid laws.

Bureaucracy (Weber)

Ideal type of organisation: hierarchy, formal rules, impersonality, merit-based advancement, division of labour. Strength: predictability and fairness. Weakness: rigidity, red tape, goal displacement when rules become ends in themselves.

4. Behavioural and Human Relations Approaches

The Hawthorne studies highlighted that social factors and attention to workers affect productivity. Later theories emphasised needs and motivation:

  • Maslow – Hierarchy of needs (physiological → safety → social → esteem → self-actualisation); higher needs motivate once lower ones are reasonably satisfied.
  • McGregor Theory X / Theory Y – Theory X assumes dislike of work and need for control; Theory Y assumes capacity for self-direction and responsibility. Manager assumptions shape behaviour (self-fulfilling prophecy).
  • Herzberg – Hygiene factors (pay, conditions, policy) prevent dissatisfaction but do not strongly motivate; motivators (achievement, recognition, work itself, responsibility, growth) drive satisfaction.

5. Contingency and Systems Views

Contingency approach – There is no one best way to manage. Effective practice depends on situational factors (environment, technology, size, strategy, people).

The systems approach views the organisation as interrelated parts exchanging inputs and outputs with the external environment. Open systems must adapt to survive. These views caution against universal “best practices” without context.

6. Leadership – Beyond Style Labels

  • Transactional leadership – Exchange-based: contingent reward and management by exception. Maintains stability and performance against standards.
  • Transformational leadership – Inspires followers to transcend self-interest via idealised influence, inspirational motivation, intellectual stimulation and individualised consideration. Associated with change and higher commitment.

Modern discussions also include servant leadership, authentic leadership and shared/team leadership. Effective leadership is often contingent on followers, task and context.

7. Ethics and Social Responsibility

Ethical frameworks used in analysis include utilitarianism (greatest good), rights, justice and care. Organisations face pressures for corporate social responsibility (CSR) and ESG (environmental, social, governance) performance. Managers need processes for ethical decision-making and a culture that supports speaking up.

Exam strategy: For theory questions, define → explain → apply to an organisation or scenario → critically evaluate (strengths, limits, modern relevance). Avoid pure description without critique or application.
