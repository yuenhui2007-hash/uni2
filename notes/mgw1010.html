MGW1010 – Introduction to Management

Degree-level: critical evaluation of classical and behavioural theory, contingency logic, leadership, ethics

1. Management as a Process

Management is the process of planning, organising, leading and controlling resources to achieve organisational goals effectively (goal attainment) and efficiently (resource-minimising).

Degree exams expect more than listing the four functions: apply them to scenarios, and discuss tensions (e.g. short-term efficiency vs long-term effectiveness; control vs innovation).

2. Classical Theory — Ideas and Critique

Scientific Management (Taylor): task specialisation, time-and-motion study, standard methods, differential piece rates. Contribution: systematic analysis of work. Critique: mechanistic view of labour, ignores social/psychological factors, “one best way” unfit for complex knowledge work and high uncertainty.

Administrative principles (Fayol): functions of management and principles (unity of command, scalar chain, etc.). Useful descriptive framework; principles are contingent guidelines, not universal laws.

Bureaucracy (Weber): ideal type with hierarchy, formal rules, impersonality, merit recruitment. Enables predictability and scale; risks goal displacement, rigidity, and demotivation when over-formalised.

3. Behavioural Lineage

Hawthorne studies shifted attention to social relations and informal groups. Motivation theories used in exams:

  • Maslow: hierarchical needs; higher needs motivate once lower needs are reasonably met — limited empirical rigidity but useful heuristic
  • McGregor X/Y: managerial assumptions shape climate and can become self-fulfilling
  • Herzberg: hygiene factors vs motivators — design jobs for achievement, recognition, responsibility, growth, not only pay/conditions

4. Systems and Contingency Thinking

Contingency approach: there is no universally best way to manage; effectiveness depends on fit with situational variables (environment, technology, size, strategy, workforce).

Open-systems view: organisations exchange inputs/outputs with the environment and must adapt to survive. This undercuts classical universalism and frames structure/leadership as design choices.

5. Leadership

Transactional: contingent reward and corrective action — maintains performance against standards.
Transformational: idealised influence, inspirational motivation, intellectual stimulation, individualised consideration — associated with change commitment.
Higher-mark answers note that both can be useful depending on context; transformational is not always superior (e.g. stable, highly programmed tasks).

6. Ethics and Responsibility

Analytical lenses: utilitarianism, rights, justice, ethic of care. CSR/ESG extend accountability beyond short-term profit to environmental and social impacts. Exams often require application of a framework to a dilemma, not generic “be ethical” statements.

Exam technique: Structure essays as define → explain theory → apply to organisation/scenario → critically evaluate (assumptions, evidence, modern relevance) → conclude. Pure description without critique caps marks.
