BFW1001 – Foundations of Finance

Deep conceptual notes: TVM, valuation, capital budgeting, risk

1. What Finance Is About

Finance studies how individuals, firms and markets allocate scarce resources over time under uncertainty. Three big questions for a firm:

  • Investment decision – Which real assets should the firm acquire? (capital budgeting)
  • Financing decision – How should the firm raise funds? (debt vs equity, capital structure)
  • Payout / working capital – How to manage short-term resources and return cash to owners

The guiding principle is to maximise the value of the firm (or shareholder wealth) in a way consistent with risk and return.

2. Time Value of Money – Conceptual Foundation

A sum of money available today is worth more than the same sum in the future because (1) it can be invested to earn a return, (2) inflation erodes purchasing power, and (3) future cash flows are uncertain (risk).

Interest (or required return) is the “price” of transferring money across time. Compounding moves value forward; discounting moves value back to the present. Present value is the fundamental building block of almost all valuation in finance.

Key Variables

  • PV – present value (today’s worth)
  • FV – future value
  • r – interest / discount rate per period
  • n – number of periods
  • PMT – constant periodic cash flow (annuity)

Core Formulas

Ordinary annuity = payments at end of each period. Annuity due = payments at beginning (slightly higher PV/FV). Always match the period of r with the period of n (e.g. monthly rate with monthly periods).

3. Capital Budgeting – Deep Logic

Capital budgeting decides which long-term projects to accept. Cash flows should be incremental (with-project minus without-project), after-tax, and should include side effects (cannibalisation, synergy) and opportunity costs. Sunk costs are ignored; financing costs are reflected in the discount rate, not double-counted in cash flows.

NPV (preferred method)

NPV is the increase in wealth from undertaking the project. A positive NPV means the project earns more than the opportunity cost of capital. Decision rule: accept independent projects with NPV > 0; for mutually exclusive projects, choose the highest NPV (given comparable scale/risk).

I₀ = 80,000; CF₁=30,000; CF₂=35,000; CF₃=40,000; r=10%
PV of inflows = 27,273 + 28,926 + 30,053 = 86,252
NPV = +6,252 → Accept (creates RM6,252 of value)

IRR

IRR is the discount rate that sets NPV = 0. Accept if IRR > required return. IRR is intuitive (a rate of return) but can mislead with non-conventional cash flows (multiple IRRs) or mutually exclusive projects of different size/timing (NPV is more reliable).

Payback Period

Time to recover the initial investment from project cash flows. Simple and emphasises liquidity, but ignores time value (unless discounted payback is used) and cash flows after the cutoff. Best used as a secondary screen, not the primary decision rule.

4. Risk and Return – Conceptual Core

Risk–return trade-off – Investors require higher expected returns to bear higher risk. Without extra expected return, rational investors would not hold riskier assets.

Expected return is the probability-weighted average of possible outcomes. Risk in finance is often measured by dispersion of returns (variance / standard deviation) or, for diversified investors, by systematic risk (beta) relative to the market.

  • Unsystematic (idiosyncratic) risk – firm-specific; can be reduced by diversification
  • Systematic (market) risk – affects many assets together; cannot be diversified away; compensated by higher expected return

In well-functioning markets, only systematic risk is “priced”. That idea underpins models such as the CAPM (optional depth at this level): expected return rises linearly with beta.

5. Financial System – Role and Structure

The financial system channels funds from savers to productive users, provides risk-sharing, liquidity and information. Key parts:

  • Financial markets – money market (short-term instruments) and capital market (long-term debt and equity)
  • Financial institutions – banks, insurers, funds, etc., that intermediate and transform risk/maturity
  • Instruments – equity, debt, hybrids, derivatives
  • Primary market – new issuance; secondary market – trading of existing claims (provides liquidity and price discovery)
