ACW1120 – Financial Accounting 1

1. Full adjusting-entry case (detailed)

Facts at 31 December (year-end):

  • Prepaid insurance RM7,200 paid on 1 September for 12 months
  • On 1 November received RM12,000 for 6 months’ consulting (recorded as Unearned revenue)
  • Staff salaries earned but unpaid RM3,600
  • Work completed for a client RM4,200 not yet billed
  • Equipment bought 1 January: cost RM90,000, residual RM9,000, useful life 9 years, straight-line, full year

Required: (a) all adjusting journals with workings; (b) net impact on profit; (c) carrying amount of equipment at 31 Dec.

(a) Workings
Insurance: used Sep–Dec = 4 months → 7,200 × 4/12 = 2,400
Consulting earned: Nov–Dec = 2 months → 12,000 × 2/6 = 4,000
Depreciation: (90,000 − 9,000) / 9 = 9,000

Journals
Dr Insurance expense 2,400    Cr Prepaid insurance 2,400
Dr Unearned revenue 4,000    Cr Service revenue 4,000
Dr Salaries expense 3,600    Cr Salaries payable 3,600
Dr Accounts receivable 4,200    Cr Service revenue 4,200
Dr Depreciation expense 9,000    Cr Accumulated depreciation – Equipment 9,000

(b) Net impact on profit
Revenue items: +4,000 +4,200 = +8,200
Expense items: −2,400 −3,600 −9,000 = −15,000
Net: profit decreases by RM6,800

(c) Carrying amount
Cost 90,000 − Accum. dep. 9,000 = RM81,000
Examiner wants: month fractions shown · no Cash in adjustments · Accum. Dep. (not Equipment) credited · profit impact with clear sign · CA = cost − accum. dep.

2. Conceptual Framework essay (model ~450 words)

“Explain the objective of general-purpose financial reporting and the fundamental qualitative characteristics. A listed company omits disclosure of a related-party loan default of RM120,000. Total assets RM80 million. Discuss whether the omission is material.”

The objective of general-purpose financial reporting in the IASB Conceptual Framework is to provide financial information about the reporting entity that is useful to existing and potential investors, lenders and other creditors in making decisions relating to providing resources to the entity. Those decisions include buying, selling or holding equity and debt instruments, and providing or settling loans and other forms of credit. Primary users also use the information to assess management’s stewardship of the entity’s economic resources.

For information to be useful it must be relevant and a faithful representation of what it purports to represent. Relevance means the information is capable of making a difference to decisions because it has predictive value, confirmatory value, or both. Materiality is an entity-specific aspect of relevance: information is material if omitting, misstating or obscuring it could reasonably be expected to influence decisions that primary users make on the basis of the financial report. Faithful representation means the depiction is as complete, neutral and free from error as practicable. Neutrality requires freedom from bias in selection or presentation.

Enhancing qualitative characteristics — comparability, verifiability, timeliness and understandability — improve usefulness when the fundamental characteristics are present, but they cannot compensate for a lack of relevance or faithful representation.

Application to the omission: Quantitatively, RM120,000 is only 0.15% of total assets, which might appear immaterial under a purely size-based rule of thumb. However, materiality is qualitative as well as quantitative. A related-party loan default raises questions about governance, related-party oversight, credit risk and the integrity of management’s stewardship. Primary users (especially lenders and equity investors) could reasonably change their assessment of risk and of the reliability of other reported amounts if they knew of the default. Nature and context therefore make the item material despite its small size relative to assets.

Conclusion: The omission is material on qualitative grounds. The company should disclose the default (and account for any loss appropriately). Relying only on a percentage-of-assets threshold would be inconsistent with the Framework’s definition of materiality.
Examiner wants: accurate Framework wording · fundamental vs enhancing not mixed · both quantitative and qualitative materiality · clear conclusion on the given scenario.

3. Retail COGS + gross profit (periodic)

Opening inventory 25,000; Purchases 110,000; Purchase returns 6,000; Purchase discounts 2,000; Freight-in 4,500; Closing inventory 23,000; Sales 198,000; Sales returns 8,000; Sales discounts 3,000. Calculate net sales, net purchases, COGS, gross profit and gross margin %.

Net sales = 198,000 − 8,000 − 3,000 = 187,000
Net purchases = 110,000 − 6,000 − 2,000 + 4,500 = 106,500
COGS = 25,000 + 106,500 − 23,000 = 108,500
Gross profit = 187,000 − 108,500 = 78,500
Gross margin % = 78,500 / 187,000 × 100% ≈ 42.0%
Examiner wants: freight-in included in purchases cost · discounts and returns treated correctly · GP% on net sales not gross sales.

BFW1001 – Foundations of Finance

1. NPV + IRR logic + ranking conflict

Project A: I₀=50,000; CF=30,000; 28,000; 10,000. Project B: I₀=120,000; CF=50,000; 55,000; 60,000. r=10%. Compute both NPVs. Which do you choose if mutually exclusive? Why might IRR rankings differ?

Project A
PV = 30k/1.1 + 28k/1.21 + 10k/1.331 = 27,273 + 23,140 + 7,513 = 57,926
NPVA = 57,926 − 50,000 = +7,926

Project B
PV = 50k/1.1 + 55k/1.21 + 60k/1.331 = 45,455 + 45,455 + 45,079 = 135,989
NPVB = 135,989 − 120,000 = +15,989

Decision (mutually exclusive, same risk, no capital rationing): choose B — higher NPV means more wealth created.

IRR ranking may prefer A because A is smaller and may show a higher percentage return. IRR can mis-rank projects of different scale or cash-flow timing; NPV is the value-maximising criterion.
Examiner wants: line-by-line PVs · both NPVs · explicit choice with wealth reason · one sentence on IRR scale problem.

2. Annuity + lump sum combined

You will receive RM5,000 at the end of each year for 5 years, plus an extra RM20,000 at the end of year 5. r=8%. What is the PV of this package?

PV of annuity = 5,000 × [1 − (1.08)−5]/0.08 = 5,000 × 3.9927 ≈ 19,964
PV of extra 20,000 = 20,000 / (1.08)5 = 20,000 / 1.4693 ≈ 13,612
Total PV ≈ 33,576
Examiner wants: separate annuity and lump-sum PVs then add · factor or full formula shown.

BTW1042 – Business Law

1. Detailed contract IRAC (invitation to treat + counter-offer)

A shop displays a laptop with a price tag RM3,000. Amir tells the cashier he accepts the price. Cashier says the tag is wrong and the price is RM3,400. Amir insists there is a contract at RM3,000. Advise both parties.

Issue: Was a contract formed at RM3,000 when Amir “accepted” the price tag?

Rule: Goods on display with price tags are generally an invitation to treat, not an offer (common-law principle applied in Malaysia). The customer makes the offer at the counter; the shop accepts or rejects. Acceptance must be unqualified; the shop is free to refuse an offer.

Application: The display was an invitation to treat. Amir’s statement at the cashier was an offer to buy at RM3,000. The cashier rejected that offer and indicated a different price (RM3,400), which is a counter-offer (or a rejection + new offer). No acceptance of Amir’s RM3,000 offer occurred.

Conclusion: No contract at RM3,000. The shop may sell at RM3,400 if Amir agrees; Amir is not obliged to buy.
Examiner wants: invitation to treat correctly identified · offeror/offeree roles clear · no contract conclusion explained.

2. Negligence IRAC (professional advice)

An accountant negligently prepares a tax computation for a client. The client relies on it, underpays tax, and is fined RM25,000 by the tax authority. Advise the client on a negligence claim.

Issue: Can the client recover the RM25,000 fine from the accountant in negligence?

Rule: Negligence requires (1) duty of care, (2) breach, (3) causation, (4) damage not too remote. Professionals owe a duty to clients to exercise the skill of a reasonably competent member of the profession. Pure economic loss may be recoverable where there is a special relationship, assumption of responsibility and reasonable reliance.

Application:
• Duty: accountant–client engagement creates a duty of care.
• Breach: negligent computation falls below the standard of a reasonably competent accountant (fact to be proved by evidence).
• Causation: but for the wrong computation, the underpayment and fine would not have occurred (assuming client followed the advice).
• Remoteness: financial loss of this type from negligent tax advice is reasonably foreseeable.

Conclusion: Subject to proof of breach and any contributory negligence, the client has a strong negligence claim for the fine and related losses.
Examiner wants: all four elements labelled · professional standard stated · economic loss linked to special relationship.

3. Company structure advice (detailed)

Three partners run a growing F&B business as a partnership. One wants to bring in an outside investor and limit personal liability. Compare partnership vs Sdn Bhd and recommend.

Partnership (Partnership Act 1961): No separate legal personality in the usual case; partners jointly and severally liable without limit for firm debts; agency of partners can bind the firm; admission of a new investor typically means a new partner with partnership law consequences; continuity can be disrupted by changes in membership.

Sdn Bhd (Companies Act 2016): Separate legal entity; liability of members limited to unpaid amounts on shares; perpetual succession; can issue shares to an investor (within private company limits: ≤50 members, no public offer of shares); requires at least one resident director, a company secretary, registered office, and ongoing SSM filings. Directors owe statutory and fiduciary duties.

Recommendation: Convert to / incorporate a Sdn Bhd. Limited liability and separate personality address the liability concern; share issuance is the clean mechanism for bringing in an outside investor. Advise them that banks may still request personal guarantees, and compliance costs will rise — but the structure matches their growth and risk goals better than a partnership.

ECM1953 – Principles of Economics

1. Demand shift + diagram description

Consumer incomes rise for a normal good. Explain effects on equilibrium price and quantity. Describe what your diagram would show.

For a normal good, higher income increases demand at each price → demand curve shifts right. Supply unchanged. New equilibrium: higher P* and higher Q*.

Diagram description: upward axes P and Q; downward-sloping D₁ and D₂ (D₂ right of D₁); upward-sloping S; mark E₁ and E₂; arrows showing P and Q increase; label “income ↑ → D shifts right”.
Examiner wants: shift not movement · both P and Q directions · diagram fully labelled if drawn.

2. Monetary policy transmission

Bank Negara cuts the overnight policy rate. Explain the transmission to AD and short-run output/prices. One risk?

Lower policy rate → lower market interest rates → higher interest-sensitive spending (I, durable C) and often exchange-rate effects supporting NX → AD shifts right → short-run Y↑ and P↑ along SRAS.

Risk: if the economy is already near capacity, the main effect is inflation rather than real output; or policy lag means stimulus arrives too late.

2. Hypothesis test write-up

A firm claims mean delivery time is 30 minutes. Sample n=36, x̄=32, s=6. Test H₀: μ=30 at α=5% (approx using z). Write full steps and conclusion in business language.

H₀: μ = 30    H₁: μ ≠ 30 (two-sided)
SE = 6/√36 = 1
z = (32−30)/1 = 2.00
Critical z at 5% two-sided ≈ ±1.96
|2.00| > 1.96 → reject H₀

Business conclusion: At the 5% significance level, sample evidence indicates mean delivery time differs from 30 minutes (it appears longer). Management should investigate process delays. (Note: statistical significance ≠ automatic practical importance; also check sample representativeness.)
Examiner wants: hypotheses · SE · test stat · decision rule · plain-language conclusion · optional caveat.

MGW1010 – Introduction to Management

1. Essay: Transformational vs transactional leadership (detailed plan + sample body)

Compare transactional and transformational leadership. Which is more suitable for a turnaround of a demotivated retail chain? Justify.

Introduction: Define both styles; state thesis (transformational primary for turnaround, transactional supports execution).

Transactional: leader–follower exchange — contingent reward, management by exception. Strengths: clarity, short-term performance control. Limits: may not rebuild commitment or change culture when morale is low.

Transformational: idealised influence, inspirational motivation, intellectual stimulation, individualised consideration. Strengths: reframes purpose, raises intrinsic motivation, supports change. Limits: needs credible vision; can be hollow without systems and fair rewards.

Application to demotivated retail turnaround: Staff need renewed purpose (customer experience, pride in store standards) and visible support — transformational behaviours fit. Simultaneously, transactional tools (clear KPIs, fair incentives, consistent feedback) stabilise daily performance. Contingency logic: crisis + need for culture change → lean transformational; routine compliance tasks → transactional discipline.

Conclusion: Lead with transformational vision and coaching; embed transactional performance management so change is executed, not only announced.
Examiner wants: accurate definitions · comparison · applied recommendation · contingency awareness · no “one style always best”.
